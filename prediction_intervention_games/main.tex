\subsection{The stable blanket under hidden variables} \label{app:stable_blanket_hidden}

The stable blanket \citep{pfisterStabilizingVariableSelection2021} described in \Cref{sec:stable_blanket} is defined for a fully observed graph over $(X, Y, E)$, whereas the hypothesized graph for the light tunnel (\cref{fig:causal_chambers_dags}, right) contains a hidden variable.
\Cref{sec:new_chamber_experiments} therefore uses the extension to hidden variables by \citet[Sections~3 and~6]{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026}, which we summarize in our notation.
Consider an acyclic SCM over $(X, H, Y, E)$ with hidden variables $H$, where, as in \Cref{sec:problem_setup}, $E$ is a source node whose children are the intervention targets. We work with the acyclic directed mixed graph (ADMG) $\mathcal{M}$ over $(X, Y, E)$ that contains a directed edge $i \to j$ whenever the DAG of the SCM contains a directed path from $i$ to $j$ whose intermediate nodes are all hidden, and a bidirected edge $i \leftrightarrow j$ whenever the DAG contains a path without colliders between $i$ and $j$ with edges pointing into both $i$ and $j$ whose intermediate nodes are all hidden, for example $i \leftarrow H_k \to j$ for a hidden common cause $H_k$.
Conditional independencies are read from $\mathcal{M}$ via m-separation \citep{richardsonMarkovPropertiesAcyclic2003}: a node $k$ is a collider on a path if both adjacent edges have an arrowhead at $k$ (as in $\to k \leftarrow$ or $\leftrightarrow k \leftrightarrow$), a path is m-blocked by $S$ if it contains a non-collider in $S$ or a collider $k$ such that neither $k$ nor any of its descendants is in $S$. Then, $E \msep Y \given S$ denotes that every path between $E$ and $Y$ in $\mathcal{M}$ is m-blocked by $S$.
For disjoint sets of observed nodes, m-separation in $\mathcal{M}$ is equivalent to d-separation in the DAG \citep{vermaGraphicalAspectsCausal1993}, so the Markov property of the SCM yields
\begin{equation*}
    E \msep Y \given S \quad \Longrightarrow \quad Y \indep E \given X_S,
\end{equation*}
that is, an m-separating set $S$ is invariant (Def.~\ref{def:inv_subset}).
We write $\DIS(j)$ for the district of $j$ in $\mathcal{M}$, that is, the set containing $j$ and all nodes connected to $j$ by paths of bidirected edges, and $\DIS(A) = \bigcup_{j \in A} \DIS(j)$ for a node set $A$. Then, the Markov blanket of $Y$ among the covariates is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:admg_mb}
    \MB_{\mathcal{M}}(Y) = \big( \DIS(Y) \cup \PA(\DIS(Y)) \cup \DIS(\CH(Y)) \cup \PA(\DIS(\CH(Y))) \big) \setminus \{Y, E\}
\end{equation}
\citep{richardsonMarkovPropertiesAcyclic2003}. The construction of \citet{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026} generalizes \eqref{eq:stable_blanket_decomp}, which can equivalently be obtained by deleting $\DEYforb$ from $\G$ and computing the Markov blanket of $Y$ in the resulting subgraph. 
With hidden variables, $E$ may be connected to $Y$ by paths whose intermediate nodes lie in $\DIS(Y)$ or in $\DIS(\CH(Y))$, the intervened districts and intervened sub-districts of \citet{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026}. Under the assumptions of \citet[Setting~6.1]{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026}, every intervened district and sub-district contains a collider with no directed path to $Y$. One chooses at least one such collider on each such path (\citet{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026} calls the chosen set an overall set of colliders $O$), deletes the nodes in $O$ together with their descendants, and evaluates \eqref{eq:admg_mb} in the resulting subgraph. This yields the stable frontier for $O$, a set $S$ satisfying $E \msep Y \given S$. Different choices of $O$ can yield different frontiers. When the frontier does not depend on the choice, it is called the stable blanket $\SB(Y)$; \citet{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026} gives sufficient graphical conditions for this.
 
For the light tunnel, $H$ is a hidden common cause of the sensor covariates and of $Y$ (through the unobserved measurement $\mathtt{ir\_1}$ that defines $Y$), and the follower intervenes on $\CH(E) = \{\mathtt{ir\_3}, \mathtt{vis\_3}\}$ without adding parents, so a single ADMG $\mathcal{M}$ describes training and deployment (see \Cref{sec:augmentation}).
The response and the sensor covariates are pairwise confounded by $H$, so
\begin{equation*}
    \DIS(Y) = \{Y, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}, \mathtt{ir\_3}, \mathtt{vis\_3}\},
\end{equation*}
and $\mathcal{M}$ further contains $\mathtt{RGB} \to Z$ for every $Z \in \DIS(Y)$ as well as $Y \to \mathtt{ir\_3}, \mathtt{vis\_3}$ and $E \to \mathtt{ir\_3}, \mathtt{vis\_3}$.
Because the intervened nodes lie in $\DIS(Y)$, the assumption of \citet[Section~3]{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026} that no intervention acts on the district of $Y$ fails, and we use the setting of \citet[Section~6]{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026}.
On the paths
\begin{equation*}
    E \to \mathtt{ir\_3} \leftrightarrow Y \qquad \text{and} \qquad E \to \mathtt{vis\_3} \leftrightarrow Y,
\end{equation*}
the intervened node is the only intermediate node and is a collider with no directed path to $Y$.
Every overall set of colliders therefore contains $\mathtt{ir\_3}$ and $\mathtt{vis\_3}$, and since conditioning on either node opens the corresponding path, no set containing one of them m-separates $E$ and $Y$.
We take the smallest choice, $O = \{\mathtt{ir\_3}, \mathtt{vis\_3}\}$.
Neither node has children in $\mathcal{M}$, so every path from $E$ to $Y$ begins with $E \to \mathtt{ir\_3}$ or $E \to \mathtt{vis\_3}$, followed by an edge pointing back into that node; the path is blocked at this collider, and $E \msep Y \given S$ holds for every $S \subseteq \{\mathtt{RGB}, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}\}$.
After deleting $O$ (its nodes have no descendants), the remaining graph has
\begin{equation*}
    \DIS(Y) = \{Y, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}\}, \qquad \PA(\DIS(Y)) = \{\mathtt{RGB}\}, \qquad \CH(Y) = \varnothing,
\end{equation*}
so \eqref{eq:admg_mb} yields the stable frontier
$\{\mathtt{RGB}, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}\}$.
It follows that $\{\mathtt{RGB}, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}\}$ is the largest stable frontier. The stable frontier is not unique here, as larger overall sets of colliders yield smaller frontiers. Slightly abusing notation, we write $\SB(Y)$ for the largest stable frontier $\{\mathtt{RGB}, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}\}$; this is the set used in \Cref{sec:new_chamber_experiments}.

Intuitively, $\mathtt{ir\_2}$ and $\mathtt{vis\_2}$ are informative about $Y$ through $H$ but unaffected by the interventions, whereas $\mathtt{ir\_3}$ and $\mathtt{vis\_3}$ are intervened on and confounded with $Y$; this is why $\hat{f}_{\mathtt{RGB}, \mathtt{ir\_2}, \mathtt{vis\_2}}$ improves on $\hat{f}_{\mathtt{RGB}}$ without losing stability, and SC recovers exactly this subset from data (\cref{fig:follower_chambers}).

Finally, the results of \Cref{sec:stable_blanket} carry over to the setting involving hidden variables.
Fix an overall set of colliders $O$, write $D(O) \coloneqq O \cup \DE(O)$ for the excluded covariates and $D(O)^C \coloneqq \{1, \ldots, d\} \setminus D(O)$, and let $S$ denote the stable frontier for $O$. These sets take the roles of $\DEYforb$, $\DEYforb^C$, and $\SB(Y)$.
The proof of Lem.~\ref{lem:stable_region_opt} uses the DAG only through the d-separations $Y \dsep{\G} E \given \DEYforb^C$ and $Y \dsep{\G} E \given \SB(Y)$ from Lem.~\ref{lemma:d-sep} and $j \dsep{\G} Y \given \SB(Y)$ for all $j \in \DEYforb^C \setminus \SB(Y)$, which hold by definition of $\SB(Y)$. The remaining steps use only the conditional independences implied by the global Markov property, not the graph.
The analogous m-separations $E \msep Y \given D(O)^C$, $E \msep Y \given S$, and $j \msep Y \given S$ for all $j \in D(O)^C \setminus S$ hold by the construction of \citet{xiangStableBlanketHidden2026} (Theorems~3.11 and~3.14, and their Section~6 extension), and they imply the same conditional independences because the observed distribution is Markov with respect to $\mathcal{M}$.
The part of the proof not using the graph therefore applies unchanged,
and Lem.~\ref{lem:bayes_restricted} then yields $R_e(f) \geq R_e(f_S)$ for every $\sigma(X_{D(O)^C})$-measurable $f$ and every $e \in \Env$.
Moreover, $\PA(Y) \subseteq D(O)^C$ still holds, since a parent of $Y$ in $D(O)$ would give a directed path from a collider in $O$ to $Y$.
\Cref{thm:PA_vs_SB_game}~(i) therefore extends to any $\sigma(X_{D(O)^C})$-measurable predictor, and both parts hold with unchanged proofs whenever $\PA(Y)$ is invariant, which holds if $E \msep Y \given \PA(Y)$.
In a DAG, the corresponding d-separation $E \dsep{\G} Y \given \PA(Y)$ always holds because $E$ is a source node and not a parent of $Y$; in an ADMG it can fail.

For the light tunnel, $E \msep Y \given \{\mathtt{RGB}\}$ holds by the above, so $\PA(Y) = \{\mathtt{RGB}\}$ is invariant: $f_{\SB(Y)}$ minimizes the risk in every environment among all predictors that do not use $\mathtt{ir\_3}$ or $\mathtt{vis\_3}$, and it weakly dominates $f_{\mathtt{RGB}}$ in the game \eqref{eq:pred_intervention_game} for both follower classes.


\subsection{Real-world examples.}
Prediction-intervention games arise in US consumer credit scoring, where the
score developer (such as FICO or VantageScore) takes the role of the leader and credit applicants are
followers. Credit scores predict future repayment from covariates derived
from an applicant's credit record. One covariate summarizes
authorized-user accounts: revolving accounts (typically credit cards) that the applicant may use without being liable for the charges. Regulation~B, which
implements the 1974 US Equal Credit Opportunity Act, requires lenders to
consider accounts an applicant shares with their spouse. Since credit records do not reveal which authorized users are spouses,
scoring models have traditionally treated all authorized-user accounts like
the applicant's own accounts \citep{brevoort2013credit}. This enables a
strategic intervention known as ``piggybacking'': credit repair companies
add credit applicants as authorized users (in name only, without receiving
a card) on a stranger's old, well-maintained account for a fee.\footnote{According to \citet[Footnote~2]{brevoort2013credit}, consumers pay between \$1{,}000 and \$2{,}000 for this
service, and the holder of the rented account can earn about \$200 per
month.} The account's favorable
history then enters the consumer's credit record, while their repayment
behavior remains unchanged. This is a costly intervention with the goal of
increasing the credit score, as in \cref{thm:PA_vs_SB_game}~(ii).
Using a credit scoring model that closely tracks commercial scores,
\citet{brevoort2013credit} estimate that adding one such account raises the
score of applicants with few accounts of their own and short credit
histories by around $20$ percentiles of the population score distribution,
and moves $28\%$ of ``subprime'' and $35\%$ of ``near-prime'' borrowers into
a higher credit-quality segment. FICO
announced in 2007 that its next model would exclude authorized-user
accounts, and VantageScore excluded them as well; after warnings that
exclusion might violate Regulation~B, FICO instead reduced the weight of
these accounts and VantageScore reintroduced them \citep{brevoort2013credit}.
This is an example of a prediction-intervention game in which the leader
(initially) chose to exclude a covariate from its model to protect itself against
strategic interventions by followers, even though the covariate is
predictive in some subgroups: excluding it leaves overall predictive
accuracy statistically unchanged, but significantly reduces accuracy for
applicants with two or fewer accounts of their own \citep{brevoort2013credit}.

A second example is web page ranking, where a search engine scores the
relevance of a page from covariates including its content and metadata. The
\texttt{keywords} meta tag lists terms the page owner places in the HTML. It
is invisible to visitors and can be changed independently of the page's content. After widespread ``keyword stuffing''
(filling the tag with irrelevant terms to attract traffic), Google excluded
it from its web ranking algorithm \citep{cutts2009keywords}.

The follower then reacts with an intervention on some covariates in the underlying structural causal model to maximize their own objective.
